## Working out

There are three key aspects of training: technical, psychological, and physical.[^2]

Technical training is not limited to self-belaying, whether climbing with or without technical equipment; it also covers route selection, 
navigation, and safe movement in the mountains. For that part, I strongly recommend my friend’s [company](https://www.verticalworld.net/portal/articles/article.php?id=438).

Of course, training is more than just a technical exercise. We need to look beyond the exercises themselves and pay attention to sleep, 
mental attitude, and nutrition, while building sport-specific physical conditioning based on aerobic endurance.

The most important aspect of mental preparation is learning to work with fear and risk. The goal is not to make fear disappear, 
but to respond to it rather than react to it—using imagery, self-talk practiced before the day arrives, controlled breathing, 
and a mindset that allows fear to do its job without taking over your whole day.

No matter how strong your technical and psychological preparation is, climbing summits above 5,000 m requires excellent physical preparation. 
The single most important indicator of that preparation is, without any doubt, VO2 max.

VO2 max is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in, transport, and use during intense exercise. In simple terms, 
it reflects the capacity of your lungs, heart, blood, and muscles to work together. On a mountain, 
it determines how efficiently you can walk uphill, carry a load, move through snow, and recover between efforts.

At altitude, this becomes even more important because the available oxygen decreases as you ascend. 
As barometric pressure falls, your body receives less oxygen with every breath. As a result, VO2 max declines with altitude—roughly 
by about 6–7% per 1,000 m—meaning that an effort which feels moderate at sea level can become significantly harder at 4,000–5,000 m. 

This is why a strong aerobic base matters so much. A climber with a higher VO2 max has a larger “reserve” when the oxygen available at altitude is reduced. 
The same pace, load, and technical terrain demand a smaller fraction of their maximum capacity, leaving more energy for balance, decision-making, 
safety, and the long summit day. VO2 max is therefore not simply a fitness number—it is a measure of your physiological margin in a hostile environment.

Acclimatization is essential, but it cannot fully replace aerobic conditioning. Spending time at altitude improves submaximal performance and helps the body adapt, 
yet VO2 max itself remains substantially lower than at sea level. Research also shows that people with a solid baseline endurance capacity are better prepared 
for the sustained uphill work, reduced oxygen availability, and cumulative fatigue of high-altitude mountaineering. 

In practice, preparation should include long, steady aerobic training—hiking, running, cycling, ski touring, or uphill walking with a pack. 
The aim is not only to become faster, but to build an engine that can keep working efficiently for many hours, day after day, with less oxygen.

Ultimately, VO2 max is one of the key factors that allows a climber to move safely, steadily, and with enough reserve above 5,000 m. 
Technique keeps you alive on the mountain; psychology keeps you composed; but aerobic capacity—measured in large part by VO2 max—gives you the physical freedom to climb.

Improving VO2 max requires consistent aerobic work, but not every training needs to be hard. The most effective approach combines a large foundation of easy endurance 
training with a small amount of well-controlled high-intensity interval work.

The best-known protocol for raising VO2 max is the Norwegian 4×4: four 4-minute efforts at approximately 90–95% of maximum heart rate, separated by 3 minutes of easy active recovery.

A simple training looks like this:

- Warm up easily for 10–15 minutes.

- Complete 4 repetitions of 4 minutes at a hard but controlled effort—about 8–9 out of 10 perceived exertion.

- Recover for 3 minutes between repetitions by walking, jogging, or cycling very easily.

- Cool down for 5–10 minutes.

Unfortunately, direct measurement of VO2 max using gas analysis is costly, requires a specialized workforce, a carefully controlled laboratory setting, and is time-consuming.

Fortunately, indirect tests may also be used to evaluate VO2 max.

### Cooper test

The standard Cooper test requires running—or run/walking—as far as possible in exactly 12 minutes, ideally on a flat 400 m track.

| The usual prediction equation |
|---|
| VO2 max (mL/kg/min)=(22.351×distance in km)−11.288 |
| |


The Cooper test has good group-level validity and reasonable reliability when the protocol is standardized: same track or accurately measured route, similar weather, 
familiar pacing, maximal effort, and consistent footwear. Cooper’s original work reported a correlation of about 0.90 between 12-minute distance and directly measured VO2 max.

However, its individual accuracy is limited. A recent study in collegiate athletes found that the Cooper test underestimated VO2 max by an average of 6.0 mL/kg/min, 
with 95% limits of agreement from −19.8 to +7.9 mL/kg/min. That means two people with the same Cooper result could have substantially different true VO2 max values. 
It is possible that some athletes did not exert their maximum effort during the CT12, which could have contributed to the underestimation relative to the ramp-incremental treadmill test.

### Smartwatch-based measurement of VO2 max

Fitness watches use a related physiological idea—estimate aerobic capacity from the relationship between external work (pace) and internal effort (heart rate)—but their algorithms
are more sophisticated than the Cooper 12-minute run test. They are not simply calculating VO2 max from distance covered in 12 minutes.

A smartwatch VO2 max number is still an estimate, not a direct measurement. Its accuracy depends on correct user profile data, reliable optical heart-rate readings, GPS quality, terrain, 
heat, fatigue, and an appropriate maximum-heart-rate setting.

Some watches include SpO2 sensors, which can provide useful context for acclimatization at altitude. However, the commonly documented VO2 max algorithms are based mainly on 
the relationship between pace and heart rate, rather than using wrist SpO2 as a direct input.

### How varying the duration of high-intensity intervals and the recovery periods between sets affects aerobic capacity and anaerobic endurance?

For a maximal effort, the anaerobic systems dominate only in the first roughly 75–80 seconds. A 10-second all-out effort is estimated to be about 91% anaerobic, 
while a 60-second maximal effort is about 58% anaerobic; beyond that, aerobic metabolism contributes an increasingly larger share.

| Interval type | Typical emphasis | Likely adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3–5 minutes near VO2 max | Sustained aerobic power | Strong stimulus for VO2 max and aerobic endurance |
| 30–90 seconds very hard | Mixed aerobic–anaerobic | Can develop anaerobic glycolytic capacity and tolerance to high lactate |
| 5–20 seconds all-out | A lactic power, neuromuscular recruitment | Primarily speed/power; less direct “anaerobic endurance” development |

Shorter, very intense intervals increase the anaerobic contribution to the effort, but they do not automatically improve anaerobic endurance more effectively. 
For mountaineering objective and  VO2 max development, longer intervals—typically 3–5 minutes—are usually more effective because they allow more time near the aerobic ceiling. 
Short 30–90-second efforts can be added selectively to develop anaerobic capacity and tolerance to high-intensity efforts.
