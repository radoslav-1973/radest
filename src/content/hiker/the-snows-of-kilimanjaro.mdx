Inspired by Hemingway’s The Snows of Kilimanjaro,[^1] I had carried a schoolboy dream with me for years: to climb the highest summit in Africa. In 2023, the dream finally stopped being something I had read about and became a real possibility I had to prepare for.
Of course, I could have simply flown to Kilimanjaro[^2] Airport, found a guide, and started walking. But that seemed suspiciously easy. I wanted to arrive prepared — or at least tired enough to believe I was prepared.

## A Warm-Up in the Indian Ocean

So I went first to Réunion Island, a French island in the Indian Ocean that looks like a tropical paradise from the beach and like a geological argument[^3] from above. It is dominated by two volcanoes: the dormant Piton des Neiges and the very much alive Piton de la Fournaise.
There I walking hundred of kilometers and climbed several summits:

- Les Trois Salazes,[^4] approached from Maïdo, along dramatic ridges above the cirques.
- Piton de la Fournaise,[^5] from Le Tampon — a landscape of black rock, craters, and clouds that makes you feel as though you have accidentally walked onto another planet.
- Piton des Neiges,[^6] from Cilaos. At 3,071 m, it is the highest point of Réunion and often called the “roof of the Indian Ocean.

It was excellent training. What’s more, it was a fabulous way to discover lots of beautiful places.

## Relaxing in Mayotte

On my way to Tanzania, I landed in Mayotte. I had visited the island a few weeks earlier and climbed its second-highest peak, Mont Choungui,[^7] at 594 m.

Most people start near the top, but I decided the proper approach was from the ocean coast near Tsimkoura village. The trail was flat at first, but the final climb was so steep that my dignity could not comfortably handle it. From the summit, however, Mayotte opened below me: lagoons, forests, villages, and the endless blue of the Indian Ocean. My legs were complaining. My eyes weren’t.

This time, I really deserved a proper relaxing break. I had four days until my flight to do absolutely nothing.

## Arrival in Dar es Salaam

When I finally arrived in Tanzania, I still had no idea that I was about to meet the best mountain guide in the world. I had nothing arranged in advance. My plan was to meet my guide in Moshi shortly before the trip — simply at the last minute.
Fortunately, it worked out, perhaps even better than a carefully booked itinerary would have. 
I always plan as I go along...

## We Reached the Summit on the Sixth Day

The final ascent began at dawn, as we had decided to climb to Uhuru Peak during daylight. We walked slowly toward the summit — the highest point of Mount Kilimanjaro at 5,895 m, the roof of Africa.

Above 5,000 m, every step becomes a negotiation. My legs were refusing to move. My lungs were crying out for air. My brain, deprived of oxygen, started offering unhelpful philosophical observations. The only correct response was to keep putting one foot in front of the other.

And then, eventually, there it was: the glacier at Uhuru Peak.

I had begun with a schoolboy dream inspired by a story about snow, death, and regret. I ended standing above the clouds — frozen, exhausted, and unexpectedly emotional — with a guide who had turned a last-minute arrangement into one of the best experiences of my life.

Kilimanjaro did not make me a literary hero. But it did help me understand why people keep returning to mountains: not because they are easy, but because mountains keep asking, Who are you?, and Can you become a better climber?

The next year, I returned to Kilimanjaro. Some mountains are not simply climbed once; they become a never-ending fairy tale.
