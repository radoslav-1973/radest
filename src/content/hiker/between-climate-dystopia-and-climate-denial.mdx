## Climate politics is increasingly trapped between two forms of extremism

On one side, some far-green voices present climate change as evidence that modern industrial society is fundamentally illegitimate. They may portray economic growth, private transport, air travel, industrial agriculture, nuclear power or technological development as inherently destructive. In its most extreme form, this argument becomes a dystopian vision in which prosperity, mobility and personal choice must be sharply restricted before society is considered morally responsible.

On the other side, parts of the far right deny or minimize climate change. Some reject the scientific evidence outright; others accept that warming exists but dismiss its causes, consequences or policy relevance. They often describe climate policy as a conspiracy against national sovereignty, economic freedom or ordinary citizens.

These positions appear opposed, but they can reinforce one another. Climate denial gives the most alarmist environmental voices an easy opponent: if critics refuse to acknowledge the problem, activists can present every disagreement over policy as proof of bad faith. At the same time, exaggerated climate rhetoric gives denialists a powerful political weapon. When climate policy is presented as the abolition of prosperity, opponents can portray climate action itself as an attack on modern life.

## The result is a destructive political feedback loop

The scientific question
The factual foundation should not be negotiated for political convenience. Human activities have increased atmospheric greenhouse-gas concentrations and caused substantial global warming. Climate risks are serious, and limiting further warming requires deep reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions.

The IPCC finds that pathways compatible with limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C involve reductions in fossil-fuel use, major increases in low-carbon energy, greater efficiency and electrification, and—in some pathways—carbon capture and carbon dioxide removal.

This does not mean that every prediction made by activists is correct. Scientific conclusions about warming trends and emissions do not automatically determine the best energy mix, transport policy, agricultural system or tax structure. Those questions involve engineering, economics, land use, public health, local ecology, ethics and democratic choice.

A person can accept climate science while disagreeing with a particular subsidy, ban, target date or technology policy.

## The false choice

One of the most damaging assumptions in the debate is that society must choose between environmental responsibility and technological development.

In reality, technological development is one of the principal ways to reduce emissions while preserving living standards. Solar and wind power, nuclear energy, hydroelectricity, geothermal systems, batteries, long-duration storage, heat pumps, better transmission networks, low-carbon fuels, industrial efficiency and carbon-management technologies can all have useful roles.

The IPCC describes multiple technically feasible pathways rather than a single mandatory blueprint. These pathways differ in their reliance on renewables, nuclear power, carbon capture, efficiency, electrification and behavioural change.

This matters because energy systems are geographically and economically diverse. A solution that works in a sunny desert may not be suitable for a densely populated northern country. A coastal state may combine nuclear power with desalination. A mountainous region may use hydropower. An industrial country may need nuclear energy, hydrogen and carbon capture for sectors that cannot be electrified easily.

## Technological pluralism is not an excuse for inaction. It is a condition of practical action.

Several recurring errors of far-green politics deserve attention.

First, some arguments treat economic growth as inherently incompatible with environmental protection. But poorer societies generally need more energy, better housing, reliable transport, clean water and industrial development. A serious climate strategy must make low-carbon development affordable rather than treating reduced consumption as the only acceptable solution.

Second, some activists reject nuclear power categorically despite its potential to provide low-carbon, high-reliability electricity. Nuclear energy is not risk-free and new projects can be expensive, but rejecting it everywhere can make decarbonization more difficult or more costly.

Third, some proposals underestimate infrastructure. Replacing fossil fuels with variable renewable generation requires transmission, storage, flexible demand, backup capacity and substantial industrial supply chains. These challenges are solvable, but not overnight by slogans.

Fourth, policies that impose visible costs on households without providing practical alternatives can generate a legitimate political backlash. A climate policy that makes heating, transport or food unaffordable may be technically defensible in a narrow model but politically unstable in the real world.

These criticisms do not invalidate environmentalism. They call for a more mature environmentalism: one that values clean air and climate stability while also valuing prosperity, reliability, innovation and public consent.

## The problems with far-right denial

Climate denial has a different and more fundamental weakness: it often rejects the problem before discussing possible solutions.

Scientific uncertainty is real, but uncertainty is not the same as ignorance. The existence of uncertainty about the exact magnitude, timing or regional distribution of impacts does not justify assuming that the risk is negligible. Nor does uncertainty justify delaying every response until all projections become perfectly precise.

Recent international polling shows a substantial global majority continues to regard climate change as a major threat, while concern is generally lower among right-wing populist supporters than among other groups in Europe. That political divide is important because climate skepticism is increasingly connected to partisan identity rather than only to scientific disagreement.

A conservative critique of climate policy can be valuable. It may emphasize cost, energy security, technological innovation, nuclear power, adaptation, national resilience or market mechanisms. But it becomes counterproductive when it denies basic physical evidence or treats every climate scientist, public institution and international assessment as part of a political conspiracy.

The political right does not need to accept every progressive climate program. It does need a credible climate position of its own.

## What a serious middle position looks like

A practical climate policy would contain several principles.

Accept the evidence. Climate change is real, human influence is substantial and emissions reductions are necessary.

Use every credible low-carbon technology. Solar, wind, nuclear, hydroelectricity, geothermal energy, storage, efficiency, electrification, hydrogen and carbon management should be assessed by performance rather than ideological identity.

Compare complete systems, not isolated technologies. The relevant question is not only the cost of a solar panel or reactor. It is the cost of delivering reliable electricity, heat and transport while including storage, transmission, backup, maintenance and environmental impacts.

Protect living standards. Climate policies should reduce emissions without treating poverty, limited mobility or lower energy consumption as moral achievements.

Improve resilience. Adaptation is necessary even under strong mitigation. Cities, agriculture, water systems, forests and infrastructure must prepare for unavoidable climate impacts.

Make costs transparent. Governments should publish assumptions about subsidies, financing, carbon prices, storage, grid expansion, land use and decommissioning rather than presenting politically convenient figures as universal truths.

Respect democratic legitimacy. People affected by energy infrastructure, land-use decisions and industrial transitions deserve meaningful participation. Public consent is not an obstacle to climate policy; it is part of durable climate policy.

## Beyond the culture war

The climate debate should move beyond moral absolutism and partisan polarization.

It is possible to reject climate denial without embracing apocalyptic rhetoric. It is possible to criticize expensive or coercive climate policies without rejecting climate science. It is possible to support nuclear power while caring about radioactive waste and safety. It is possible to support solar power while acknowledging land, transmission, recycling and storage challenges.

The central political task is not to discover one morally pure technology. It is to build an energy system that is cleaner, more reliable, more affordable and more resilient than the fossil-fuel system it replaces.

Far-green dystopianism and far-right climate denial each obstruct that task in different ways. The first can make climate action appear like a war on prosperity. The second can make reasonable climate action appear unnecessary. Together they polarize an issue that requires engineering, investment, patience and broad cooperation.

A serious response should therefore be neither utopian nor dismissive. It should be empirical, technologically open, economically literate and socially realistic. Climate change is a major problem—but it is not proof that human progress must end. It is a reason to improve the technologies and institutions through which progress is achieved.
